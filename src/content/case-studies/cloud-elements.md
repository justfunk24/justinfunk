## Situation

Cloud Elements had a revenue team and no enablement function. That's normal at that stage and it's fine right up until it isn't — usually the moment you hire faster than your best rep can personally mentor.

What that looks like day to day: every new seller learns a slightly different version of the product, the pitch drifts by whoever trained them, and nobody can tell whether a rep who's struggling has a skill problem or was never actually taught the thing. There was no baseline, so there was nothing to diagnose against.

## What I did

**Built the onboarding and certification program in Docebo.** Certification is the part that matters. Onboarding without a bar at the end is a reading list — people attend, everyone agrees it was useful, and nothing is verified. Making sellers demonstrate the pitch before carrying quota meant "trained" became a fact rather than an assumption.

**Administered Salesforce as the system of record.** Objects, fields, reporting, and the process design around them. Unglamorous and load-bearing: enablement can't prove it worked without clean data underneath, and a pipeline nobody trusts turns every forecast conversation into an argument about the numbers instead of about the deals.

**Wrote the operating stack down.** Not a document nobody reads — the small set of things a new person needs on day one, day thirty, and day ninety, and who owns each. In a company without an enablement function, most of this knowledge lives in a few people's heads, and it leaves when they do.

**Talked about it in public.** I presented the certification program at **Docebo Inspire 2019**. Explaining a program to a room of practitioners who build the same thing is a real test of whether the design holds up.

A functioning enablement and sales operations practice where there had been none, plus the Docebo Inspire talk.

The lesson I took forward is that building a function from zero is mostly a sequencing problem. Everything is missing at once, and the temptation is to fix the loudest gap first. Onboarding came first because it compounds — every week you delay it, another cohort learns the wrong version.
