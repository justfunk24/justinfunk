## Situation

Compensation questions are the highest-anxiety, lowest-patience questions a seller ever asks. Nobody files a ticket about a comp plan out of curiosity. They file it because a deal is closing, the math doesn't look right, and they want an answer today.

At Twilio those tickets were climbing. The enablement library had plenty in it; the plan documents existed and were current. But a seller with a specific question about their own deal couldn't get from that question to the answer without asking a person, and 400+ sellers asking people doesn't scale. The ticket volume was the symptom. Findability was the disease.

## What I did

**Started from the search log, not the content inventory.** The usual move is to audit what exists and reorganize it. That tells you what the business published, not what anyone was looking for. Reading the failed searches and the actual ticket text told me which questions kept getting asked and which words sellers used to ask them, and those words were rarely the words on the documents.

**Rebuilt around the question rather than the department.** Content had been organized the way the org chart was organized, which makes sense to everyone except the person searching. A seller doesn't know whether their question belongs to Finance or Sales Ops. Reorganizing around the questions themselves meant the answer was reachable without knowing who owned it.

**Made governance somebody's actual job.** Enablement libraries rot quietly. A document that was right eighteen months ago is worse than no document, because it costs the same to find and it's wrong. Every page got an owner and a review date, and anything past its date got flagged rather than silently persisting.

**Measured findability, not publishing volume.** Pieces published is the easiest enablement metric to report and the least connected to whether anyone was helped. Search success rate and ticket deflection are harder to move and actually mean something.

## Outcome

Compensation support tickets fell **60% year over year**.

The part worth generalizing: the library wasn't missing content, and adding more would have made it worse. That's true of most enablement problems I've seen since, and it's the same failure mode I now watch for in knowledge bases feeding AI agents. A retrieval layer over badly organized content inherits the disorganization and answers confidently anyway.
