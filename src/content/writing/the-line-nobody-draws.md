There's a moment in every platform evaluation where someone says they know a system, and everyone in the room silently decides whether to believe them.

I've been on both sides of that moment. I've watched a consultant describe a Genesys migration with total confidence and no scar tissue, and I've been the person who had to admit that what I knew about a platform came from competitive decks rather than a deployment. The second one felt worse in the moment and worked out better every single time.

Here's what I think is actually going on. "Familiar with" and "built on" are separated by roughly the entire distance of a project. Familiar with means you can hold your own in an evaluation, position it against alternatives, and ask the questions that matter. Built on means you've hit the undocumented behavior at 2am, found the integration that quietly rate-limits, and learned which of the vendor's promises survive a real support org. Both are worth having. They are not the same thing, and a room can usually tell within two follow-up questions.

The reason people blur the line is understandable — a longer platform list looks like a stronger candidate. But a list that can't survive scrutiny doesn't fail gently. It fails on the one platform the buyer happens to know cold, and it takes everything else you said down with it.

What I've found is that drawing the line yourself is the cheapest credibility you can buy. Say plainly which four you've built on and which four you can evaluate, and the four you claim get taken seriously in a way that eight never would. You also get a much better conversation, because now the buyer can tell you what they actually need instead of guessing at what you can actually do.

There's a version of this industry where that's just how everyone talks — where the delivery partner says "we haven't done this one, here's who has" and it reads as strength rather than a lost deal. I don't think that's naive. I think the buyers are getting better at detecting the alternative.

What's the platform you know well enough to evaluate but wouldn't want to deploy alone — and would you say so in a pitch?
